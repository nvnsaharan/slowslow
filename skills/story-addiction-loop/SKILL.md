# Story Addiction Loop

A loop that keeps attention the way a casino hand does: care, wonder, get surprised, immediately want the next hand. Good stories have one loop. Great stories run many, each opening before the last one fully closes.

This governs the shape of a story. Voice and banned phrases still come from `x-marketing-genius` and `antislop-copywriting`. Facts must be real: never invent stakes, numbers, quotes or reactions to make a loop work.

## The four steps

### 1. Stakes
Nobody gets a dopamine hit from something they do not care about. Stakes need all three:
1. **A character to root for** (a person, a team, "I", a product with a face).
2. **Something at risk** (money, time, reputation, a launch, a relationship, a deadline).
3. **Urgency** (why it matters now, a clock, a limit, a cost of waiting).

Test: if one of the three is missing, the opening is information, not a story. Name all three in one or two sentences.

### 2. The Big Question
Give just enough context that one specific, juicy question forms in the reader's head. Stakes buy attention; the question buys curiosity.
- Casino version: which card comes next.
- Good: "Will the migration finish before the old system falls over?" "Did the model really ignore the instruction?"
- Weak: vague questions ("what happens next?"), or a question the reader can already answer.

Write the question down in one line. If you cannot, the setup is too loose or too long.

### 3. The Headfake
The hook. Stakes and the Big Question lead the reader to assume A. Snap the story to B.
- It must contrast with what a normal viewer would expect, and it must be true and earned: the clues for B were fair.
- A headfake is not a fake-out lie, a clickbait gap with no payoff, or a twist with no consequence. The reveal must change what the stakes mean.
- Ask: what would the reader assume here? Then find the true thing that contradicts it.

### 4. The Rehook
Restart the loop fast. As the headfake lands, open the next stakes or the next question before the reader can leave.
- The answer to question one should raise question two.
- Cascade: stack loops so each reveal is also the setup of the next.
- End a section, tweet, scene or chapter on an open question, not a summary.

## Process when writing
1. Name the character, the thing at risk and the clock (Stakes).
2. Write the Big Question as one line.
3. List what the audience will assume. Pick the true B that contradicts it (Headfake). If there is no real B, say so and tighten the piece instead of inventing one.
4. Decide the Rehook: what question does the reveal open?
5. Draft, then walk the loop once per section.

## Process when reviewing
Mark each section: Stakes present? Question clear? Contrast real? Next hook opened? Report the weakest step and one concrete fix. Typical failures:
- Context first, stakes never (reader asks "why should I care").
- Question answered in the first line (nothing to wait for).
- Twist the reader could have guessed, or one that cheats.
- Story ends loops instead of chaining them.

## Fits by format
- **X thread:** tweet 1 = stakes plus question, middle tweets = progress toward the headfake, reveal near the end, last line opens the next loop or a follow-up post.
- **Video or Reel:** stakes in the first seconds, headfake around the midpoint, rehook as a cut or caption before the end.
- **Launch or case study:** the headfake is the surprising reason behind the product or the result, not the feature list.
- **Founder or engineering story:** "we expected X, got Y" is a native headfake; keep real numbers and real mistakes.
- **Interview answers (STAR):** Situation = stakes, Task = question, Action and surprise = headfake, Result that opens the next challenge = rehook.

## Output format
When asked to write: give the loop map first (Stakes / Big Question / Headfake / Rehook, one line each), then the draft. When asked to review: the four-step checklist with the weakest step first.
